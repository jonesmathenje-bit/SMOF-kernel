# SRM-0011 — Transition Stability Mathematics

**Document ID:** SRM-0011
**Title:** Transition Stability Mathematics
**System:** SMOF Kernel
**Domain:** State Transition Mathematics
**Status:** Canonical Draft
**Predecessors:** SRM-0002, SRM-0003, SRM-0004, SRM-0005, SRM-0006, SRM-0007, SRM-0008, SRM-0009, SRM-0010

---

## 1. Purpose

SRM-0011 defines the mathematical foundations of stability within the SMOF state-transition framework.

The specification formalizes stability as a property of states, trajectories, invariant sets, and attractors under repeated application of transition operators.

The purpose of this specification is to establish a canonical mathematical language for determining whether perturbations of a state remain controlled under transition dynamics.

SRM-0011 extends the transition mathematics established by SRM-0002 through SRM-0010.

---

## 2. Scope

SRM-0011 defines:

* transition stability;
* state stability;
* trajectory stability;
* local stability;
* global stability;
* Lyapunov stability;
* asymptotic stability;
* exponential stability;
* uniform stability;
* practical stability;
* stability of invariant sets;
* stability of attractors;
* basin-relative stability;
* perturbation distance;
* stability neighborhoods;
* forward stability;
* stability preservation under composition;
* stability under conjugacy;
* stability relations between transitions and invariant structures.

This specification does not define numerical algorithms for estimating stability from experimental data.

## 3. Mathematical Context

Let a SMOF transition system be represented by

[
\mathcal{R}=(S,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{O})
]

where:

* (S) is the state space;
* (\mathcal{T}) is the family of admissible transitions;
* (\mathcal{O}) is the family of transition operators.

A transition is represented by

[
T:S\rightarrow S.
]

For a state (x\in S), the forward trajectory is

[
x_{n+1}=T(x_n),
]

with

[
x_n=T^n(x_0).
]

SRM-0007 defines reachability and orbit mathematics.

SRM-0008 defines periodicity and recurrence.

SRM-0009 defines invariant sets.

SRM-0010 defines attractors.

SRM-0011 defines the stability properties of these structures.

---

## 4. State Perturbation

Let (x\in S) be a reference state and let (y\in S) be a perturbed state.

A perturbation is represented by

[
\delta(x,y).
]

When (S) possesses a metric (d),

[
\delta(x,y)=d(x,y).
]

The perturbed state (y) is said to be within radius (\epsilon) of (x) when

[
d(x,y)<\epsilon.
]

The corresponding neighborhood is

[
B_\epsilon(x)
=============

{y\in S:d(x,y)<\epsilon}.
]

---

## 5. Stability Principle

A transition (T) is stable at a state (x) if sufficiently small perturbations of (x) produce trajectories that remain sufficiently close to the trajectory generated by (x).

The fundamental stability relation is

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon
]

for the required range of (n), provided that

[
d(x,y)<\delta.
]

Thus stability establishes a controlled relationship between initial perturbation and subsequent transition evolution.

---

## 6. Lyapunov Stability

Let (x^\ast\in S) be a fixed point satisfying

[
T(x^\ast)=x^\ast.
]

The fixed point (x^\ast) is Lyapunov stable if

[
\forall\epsilon>0,\ \exists\delta>0
]

such that

[
d(x,x^\ast)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)<\epsilon
]

for every

[
n\geq0.
]

Therefore,

[
x^\ast\text{ stable}
\iff
\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0
\forall n\geq0:
d(x,x^\ast)<\delta
\Rightarrow
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)<\epsilon.
]

---

## 7. Stability of a General State

For a general state (x), stability is defined relative to its reference trajectory.

Let

[
x_n=T^n(x).
]

A state (x) is stable under (T) when for every (\epsilon>0) there exists (\delta>0) such that

[
d(x,y)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon
]

for all

[
n\geq0.
]

This definition does not require (x) to be a fixed point.

---

## 8. Trajectory Stability

The orbit of (x) is

[
\operatorname{Orb}_T(x)
=======================

{T^n(x):n\geq0}.
]

A trajectory is stable when perturbations of its initial condition generate trajectories that remain close to the reference trajectory.

Thus,

[
\forall\epsilon>0,\ \exists\delta>0
]

such that

[
d(x,y)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon
]

for all (n\geq0).

Trajectory stability is therefore a property of the orbit rather than only of an individual state.

---

## 9. Local Stability

A state (x^\ast) is locally stable if stability holds within some neighborhood

[
U\subseteq S
]

containing (x^\ast).

There exists

[
r>0
]

such that

[
B_r(x^\ast)\subseteq U
]

and every initial condition in this neighborhood satisfies the required stability relation.

Local stability does not imply global stability.

---

## 10. Global Stability

A state (x^\ast) is globally stable if the stability condition holds for every admissible initial state in (S).

Thus,

[
\forall x\in S
]

the trajectory generated by (x) satisfies the required stability relation with respect to (x^\ast).

Global stability is therefore stronger than local stability.

---

## 11. Asymptotic Stability

A fixed point (x^\ast) is asymptotically stable when it is Lyapunov stable and trajectories beginning sufficiently close to (x^\ast) converge to (x^\ast).

Thus,

[
d(x,x^\ast)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)<\epsilon
]

for all (n\geq0), together with

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}T^n(x)=x^\ast.
]

Therefore,

[
\text{asymptotic stability}
===========================

\text{stability}
+
\text{attraction}.
]

---

## 12. Exponential Stability

A fixed point (x^\ast) is exponentially stable if there exist constants

[
C>0
]

and

[
0<\lambda<1
]

such that

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)
\leq
C\lambda^n d(x,x^\ast)
]

for all sufficiently small perturbations.

Equivalently, writing

[
\lambda=e^{-\alpha},
\qquad
\alpha>0,
]

gives

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)
\leq
Ce^{-\alpha n}d(x,x^\ast).
]

Exponential stability provides a quantitative convergence rate.

---

## 13. Uniform Stability

A transition system is uniformly stable over a set (A\subseteq S) if the same perturbation tolerance can be selected uniformly for every reference state in (A).

That is,

[
\forall\epsilon>0,\ \exists\delta>0
]

such that

[
x\in A,\quad d(x,y)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon
]

for all

[
n\geq0.
]

Uniformity prevents the stability tolerance from depending on the particular reference state.

---

## 14. Practical Stability

A state may be considered practically stable when perturbations remain bounded by a prescribed operational tolerance.

Let

[
\eta>0
]

be an admissible tolerance.

A state (x) is practically stable if there exists

[
\delta>0
]

such that

[
d(x,y)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\eta
]

for all relevant (n).

Practical stability is useful when exact mathematical convergence is unnecessary but bounded deviation is required.

---

## 15. Stability of Invariant Sets

Let

[
A\subseteq S
]

be invariant under (T).

Then

[
T(A)\subseteq A.
]

The set (A) is stable if sufficiently small perturbations of points in (A) remain sufficiently close to (A) under forward iteration.

Define the distance from a point (x) to (A) by

[
d(x,A)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a).
]

Then (A) is stable if

[
\forall\epsilon>0,\ \exists\delta>0
]

such that

[
d(x,A)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),A)<\epsilon
]

for all

[
n\geq0.
]

---

## 16. Stability of Attractors

Let (A) be an attractor established according to SRM-0010.

An attractor is stable when trajectories beginning sufficiently close to (A) remain close to (A) and converge toward it.

Thus,

[
d(x,A)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),A)<\epsilon
]

for all (n\geq0), together with

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(x),A)=0.
]

Therefore, an asymptotically stable attractor satisfies

[
\text{stability}
+
\text{attraction}.
]

---

## 17. Basin-Relative Stability

Let

[
B(A)
]

denote the basin of attraction of an attractor (A).

Stability may be defined relative to the basin:

[
x\in B(A).
]

For sufficiently small perturbations that remain within the basin,

[
y\in B(A),
]

the perturbed trajectory satisfies

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(y),A)=0.
]

Basin-relative stability distinguishes perturbations that preserve asymptotic behavior from perturbations that cause a transition to another invariant structure.

---

## 18. Forward Stability

SMOF transition dynamics are primarily defined through forward transition.

A state or set is forward stable when the stability condition holds for

[
n\geq0.
]

Thus,

[
T^0,T^1,T^2,\ldots
]

are the relevant transition operators.

Forward stability does not require the existence of inverse transitions.

---

## 19. Stability and Transition Composition

Let

[
T_1:S\rightarrow S
]

and

[
T_2:S\rightarrow S.
]

Their composition is

[
T=T_2\circ T_1.
]

If both transitions preserve a stability neighborhood under compatible bounds, their composition preserves stability over the corresponding composed domain.

The resulting trajectory is

[
T^n(x)
======

(T_2\circ T_1)^n(x).
]

Stability must therefore be evaluated under the composed transition structure rather than independently at only one transition step.

---

## 20. Stability Under Conjugacy

Let

[
T:S\rightarrow S
]

and

[
U:S'\rightarrow S'
]

be conjugate through a bijection

[
h:S\rightarrow S'
]

such that

[
U\circ h=h\circ T.
]

Then

[
U^n\circ h=h\circ T^n.
]

If (h) and (h^{-1}) preserve the relevant distance structure, stability properties of corresponding states are preserved under the conjugacy.

Thus,

[
x^\ast
\longleftrightarrow
h(x^\ast)
]

provides a correspondence between the stability structures of the two transition systems.

---

## 21. Perturbation Propagation

Define the perturbation at step (n) by

[
e_n=d(T^n(x),T^n(y)).
]

The initial perturbation is

[
e_0=d(x,y).
]

A stable system constrains the sequence

[
{e_n}_{n\geq0}.
]

Possible stability behaviors include:

[
e_n\leq C e_0,
]

[
e_n\rightarrow0,
]

or

[
e_n\leq C\lambda^n e_0,
\qquad
0<\lambda<1.
]

These correspond respectively to bounded, asymptotically decaying, and exponentially decaying perturbations.

---

## 22. Neutral Stability

A state may exhibit neutral stability when perturbations remain bounded without necessarily converging to zero.

A representative condition is

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
\leq
C,d(x,y).
]

Neutral stability therefore permits persistent perturbations.

It differs from asymptotic stability because

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
]

need not equal zero.

---

## 23. Instability

A state (x^\ast) is unstable if the Lyapunov stability condition fails.

Equivalently, there exists

[
\epsilon_0>0
]

such that for every

[
\delta>0
]

there exists an initial state (x) satisfying

[
d(x,x^\ast)<\delta
]

and some

[
n\geq0
]

for which

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(x^\ast))\geq\epsilon_0.
]

Instability therefore represents the failure of perturbation containment.

---

## 24. Stability Classification

SMOF stability may be classified as follows:

[
\text{Stability}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Local Stability}\
\text{Global Stability}\
\text{Uniform Stability}\
\text{Neutral Stability}\
\text{Asymptotic Stability}\
\text{Exponential Stability}\
\text{Practical Stability}\
\text{Instability}
\end{cases}
]

These categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

For example,

[
\text{exponential stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{asymptotic stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{Lyapunov stability}.
]

---

## 25. Stability Neighborhood

For a stable state (x^\ast), define a stability neighborhood

[
N_\delta(x^\ast)
================

{x\in S:d(x,x^\ast)<\delta}.
]

The neighborhood is admissible when every state inside it satisfies the required stability condition.

Thus,

[
N_\delta(x^\ast)
]

defines the region of initial perturbations for which stability is guaranteed.

---

## 26. Stability Radius

The stability radius measures the maximum perturbation scale for which a prescribed stability condition remains valid.

Define

[
r_{\mathrm{stab}}(x^\ast)
=========================

\sup{r>0:\text{the required stability property holds on }B_r(x^\ast)}.
]

The stability radius may be finite or infinite.

A large stability radius indicates greater tolerance to perturbation.

---

## 27. Stability Margin

Let (\epsilon) be the maximum allowable deviation.

A stability margin may be represented by

[
m=\epsilon-\sup_{n\geq0}d(T^n(x),T^n(y)).
]

When

[
m>0,
]

the trajectory remains within the prescribed tolerance.

When

[
m=0,
]

the trajectory reaches the tolerance boundary.

When

[
m<0,
]

the stability requirement is violated.

---

## 28. Stability of Periodic Orbits

Let (x) be periodic with period (p):

[
T^p(x)=x.
]

The periodic orbit is

[
\mathcal{O}_p(x)
================

{x,T(x),\ldots,T^{p-1}(x)}.
]

The orbit is stable when sufficiently small perturbations of any point on the orbit remain close to the corresponding periodic trajectory.

Thus,

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon
]

for sufficiently small initial perturbations.

Periodic stability is therefore defined over the complete orbit rather than a single point.

---

## 29. Stability of Recurring States

If a state is recurrent according to SRM-0008, stability concerns the behavior of perturbations around its recurrent trajectory.

Let

[
n_k\rightarrow\infty
]

be a sequence satisfying

[
T^{n_k}(x)\rightarrow x.
]

A recurrent structure is stable when perturbations preserve the corresponding recurrent behavior within the required tolerance.

Stability therefore distinguishes controlled recurrence from recurrence that is destroyed by arbitrarily small perturbations.

---

## 30. Stability and Invariant Sets

Let (A\subseteq S) be invariant.

Stability and invariance represent distinct properties:

[
\text{invariance}
\neq
\text{stability}.
]

Invariance requires

[
T(A)\subseteq A.
]

Stability requires that nearby states remain close to (A).

An invariant set may therefore be unstable.

Conversely, a neighborhood may exhibit transient stability without containing an invariant set.

---

## 31. Stability and Attractors

An attractor combines invariant structure with attraction.

Stability adds the requirement that sufficiently small perturbations remain controlled.

The conceptual relation is

[
\text{stable attractor}
=======================

\text{invariant structure}
+
\text{stability}
+
\text{attraction}.
]

This relation connects SRM-0009, SRM-0010, and SRM-0011.

---

## 32. Lyapunov Function

Let

[
V:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}
]

be a scalar function satisfying

[
V(x^\ast)=0
]

and

[
V(x)>0
]

for

[
x\neq x^\ast
]

in a neighborhood of (x^\ast).

The transition difference is

[
\Delta V(x)
===========

V(T(x))-V(x).
]

If

[
\Delta V(x)\leq0,
]

then (V) does not increase under transition.

This provides a mathematical mechanism for establishing stability.

---

## 33. Strict Lyapunov Decrease

If

[
\Delta V(x)<0
]

for all

[
x\neq x^\ast
]

within a neighborhood, then the Lyapunov function strictly decreases under transition.

A strict decrease condition provides a stronger basis for asymptotic stability.

Thus,

[
V(T(x))<V(x)
]

for

[
x\neq x^\ast.
]

---

## 34. Exponential Lyapunov Condition

A stronger condition is

[
V(T(x))
\leq
\lambda V(x)
]

where

[
0<\lambda<1.
]

Repeated transition gives

[
V(T^n(x))
\leq
\lambda^n V(x).
]

Therefore,

[
V(T^n(x))\rightarrow0.
]

This establishes exponential decay of the Lyapunov measure.

---

## 35. Stability and Jacobians

Suppose the transition operator is differentiable:

[
T:S\rightarrow S.
]

The local linearization near (x^\ast) is governed by the Jacobian

[
J_T(x^\ast).
]

For a fixed point,

[
T(x^\ast)=x^\ast.
]

The linearized perturbation satisfies approximately

[
\delta x_{n+1}
==============

J_T(x^\ast)\delta x_n.
]

Therefore,

[
\delta x_n
\approx
J_T(x^\ast)^n\delta x_0.
]

The spectral behavior of (J_T(x^\ast)) provides local information concerning stability.

---

## 36. Spectral Stability Criterion

Let

[
\rho(J_T(x^\ast))
]

denote the spectral radius of the Jacobian.

A standard sufficient local condition for discrete-time asymptotic stability is

[
\rho(J_T(x^\ast))<1.
]

If eigenvalues satisfy

[
|\lambda_i|<1
]

for every eigenvalue (\lambda_i) of (J_T(x^\ast)), perturbations decay locally under the linearized dynamics.

The condition

[
|\lambda_i|>1
]

for at least one eigenvalue indicates a locally expanding direction and may establish instability under appropriate regularity conditions.

---

## 37. Nonlinear Stability

Linearization does not completely characterize every nonlinear transition.

A nonlinear transition may satisfy

[
T(x)=x+F(x)
]

where the nonlinear term (F) cannot be neglected over the relevant state domain.

Therefore, nonlinear stability must be established using the complete transition map, Lyapunov functions, invariant regions, contraction properties, or other valid mathematical arguments.

---

## 38. Contraction Stability

A transition (T) is contractive on (A\subseteq S) if there exists

[
0\leq\lambda<1
]

such that

[
d(T(x),T(y))
\leq
\lambda d(x,y)
]

for all

[
x,y\in A.
]

After (n) transitions,

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
\leq
\lambda^n d(x,y).
]

Therefore contraction implies exponential decay of perturbation distance.

---

## 39. Stability Under Bounded Disturbance

Let a disturbed transition be represented by

[
x_{n+1}=T(x_n)+w_n
]

where (w_n) is a bounded disturbance satisfying

[
|w_n|\leq\eta.
]

A robustly stable transition system maintains bounded state deviation under bounded disturbances.

A generic bound has the form

[
d(x_n,x_n^\ast)
\leq
C\lambda^n d(x_0,x_0^\ast)
+
K\eta
]

where

[
0<\lambda<1.
]

The first term decays while the second term represents the persistent disturbance bound.

---

## 40. Robust Stability

A transition system is robustly stable if its stability property persists under a specified class of admissible transition perturbations.

Let

[
\widetilde{T}=T+\Delta T.
]

Robust stability requires that sufficiently small admissible perturbations

[
|\Delta T|<\eta
]

do not destroy the specified stability property.

Robust stability therefore concerns stability of the transition law itself.

---

## 41. Stability of Transition Operators

Let

[
T_1,T_2:S\rightarrow S.
]

The operators are stability-equivalent over (A\subseteq S) when they preserve the same stability class for corresponding states or invariant structures within (A).

This relation is stronger than equality of individual transition outputs and concerns the dynamical behavior induced by the operators.

---

## 42. Stability Preservation

Let (P) denote a stability property.

A transition transformation

[
\Phi:T\mapsto T'
]

preserves (P) if

[
P(T)\Rightarrow P(T').
]

For conjugate systems, the corresponding preservation relation is

[
P(T,x)
\Rightarrow
P(U,h(x)).
]

This provides a structural criterion for transferring stability between equivalent transition representations.

---

## 43. Stability and Reachability

Let

[
R_T(x)
]

be the reachable set from (x).

Stability constrains how reachable sets respond to perturbations.

For nearby states (x) and (y),

[
d(x,y)<\delta
]

a stable transition system requires the corresponding reachable trajectories to remain within controlled distance.

Thus reachability and stability are related but distinct:

[
\text{reachability}
===================

\text{which states can be reached},
]

while

[
\text{stability}
================

\text{how perturbations affect reachable trajectories}.
]

---

## 44. Stability and Orbit Structure

For

[
\operatorname{Orb}_T(x)
=======================

{T^n(x):n\geq0},
]

stability concerns the behavior of nearby orbits.

Let

[
y\approx x.
]

Then stability requires

[
\operatorname{Orb}_T(y)
]

to remain close to

[
\operatorname{Orb}_T(x)
]

according to the selected stability criterion.

---

## 45. Stability and Recurrence

Recurrence concerns the return of a trajectory to neighborhoods of previously visited states.

Stability concerns the persistence of trajectory behavior under perturbation.

Therefore,

[
\text{recurrence}
\neq
\text{stability}.
]

A recurrent orbit may be stable or unstable.

A stable orbit may be recurrent, periodic, convergent, or otherwise dynamically structured.

---

## 46. Stability and Periodicity

If

[
T^p(x)=x,
]

then the orbit is periodic.

Periodicity alone does not establish stability.

The periodic orbit must separately satisfy a perturbation-containment condition.

Therefore,

[
\text{periodicity}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{stability}.
]

---

## 47. Stability Hierarchy

The principal stability hierarchy is

[
\text{Contraction}
\Rightarrow
\text{Exponential Stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{Asymptotic Stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{Lyapunov Stability}.
]

The reverse implications do not generally hold.

Likewise,

[
\text{Global Stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{Local Stability}.
]

The reverse implication does not generally hold.

---

## 48. Stability Failure Modes

A transition system may lose stability through:

1. unbounded perturbation growth;
2. loss of an invariant neighborhood;
3. destruction of an attracting structure;
4. eigenvalue crossing of the unit boundary;
5. loss of contraction;
6. basin boundary crossing;
7. transition-operator perturbation;
8. structural bifurcation;
9. accumulation of disturbances;
10. numerical or computational instability.

These failure modes are mathematically distinct and must not be conflated.

---

## 49. Stability Certification

A stability claim shall identify:

1. the reference state or invariant structure;
2. the transition operator;
3. the state-space structure;
4. the distance or perturbation measure;
5. the neighborhood of validity;
6. the stability criterion;
7. the quantifiers governing perturbation tolerance;
8. the time domain;
9. any required regularity assumptions;
10. the proof or mathematical justification.

A statement of stability without these elements is incomplete as a canonical mathematical assertion.

---

## 50. Stability Certificate

A stability certificate may be represented abstractly as

[
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{stab}}
===========================

(S,T,A,d,\epsilon,\delta,\mathcal{P})
]

where:

* (S) is the state space;
* (T) is the transition operator;
* (A) is the reference state or invariant structure;
* (d) is the distance measure;
* (\epsilon) is the admissible deviation;
* (\delta) is the initial perturbation tolerance;
* (\mathcal{P}) is the proof or stability predicate.

The certificate establishes the conditions under which the stability claim is valid.

---

## 51. Stability Predicate

Define

[
\operatorname{Stable}(T,A,\epsilon,\delta)
]

to mean that

[
d(x,A)<\delta
]

implies

[
d(T^n(x),A)<\epsilon
]

for every admissible

[
n\geq0.
]

For asymptotic stability, augment the predicate with

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(x),A)=0.
]

---

## 52. Stability Operator

The stability analysis of a transition system may be represented by an abstract operator

[
\mathfrak{S}(T,A)
]

that returns the stability classification of (A) under (T).

Thus,

[
\mathfrak{S}(T,A)
\in
{
\text{stable},
\text{unstable},
\text{asymptotically stable},
\text{exponentially stable},
\text{unknown}
}.
]

The value `unknown` indicates that the available mathematical information is insufficient to establish a classification.

---

## 53. Stability Invariant

A quantity

[
I:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
]

is a stability invariant over (A) when its preservation or monotonic behavior provides a stability guarantee.

For example,

[
I(T(x))\leq I(x)
]

may establish boundedness of a transition trajectory.

A strictly decreasing quantity may provide stronger convergence information.

---

## 54. Stability Region

Define the stability region of a reference structure (A) as

[
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{stab}}(A)
==============================

{x\in S:
\text{the required stability property holds for }x}.
]

For an asymptotically stable attractor, this region may contain its basin of attraction or a subset of that basin, depending on the precise criterion.

---

## 55. Stability Boundary

The boundary of the stability region is

[
\partial\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{stab}}(A).
]

Crossing this boundary may cause a change in dynamical behavior.

The boundary therefore separates initial conditions according to the specified stability criterion.

---

## 56. Local-to-Global Stability

Local stability provides information within a neighborhood.

Global stability requires the property to extend across the complete admissible state space.

The implication is

[
\text{global stability}
\Rightarrow
\text{local stability},
]

but generally

[
\text{local stability}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{global stability}.
]

A global result therefore requires additional mathematical structure.

---

## 57. Stability of Nested Invariant Sets

Suppose

[
A_1\subseteq A_2\subseteq S
]

and both sets are invariant.

Stability of (A_1) does not automatically imply stability of (A_2).

Each invariant set requires its own stability analysis unless a valid implication has been proved.

---

## 58. Stability of Nested Attractors

If

[
A_1\subseteq A_2,
]

the attraction and stability properties of the two sets must be distinguished.

An attractor may be stable relative to a neighborhood while another larger invariant structure remains unstable.

Therefore stability classification is attached to the specific dynamical object under consideration.

---

## 59. Stability Under Iterated Transition

Let

[
T^k
]

be the (k)-step transition.

If

[
T
]

is exponentially stable with rate (\lambda), then

[
T^k
]

inherits a corresponding exponential decay rate.

Specifically,

[
d(T^{kn}(x),T^{kn}(y))
\leq
C\lambda^{kn}d(x,y).
]

Thus stability is compatible with transition iteration.

---

## 60. Stability Under Identity

For the identity transition

[
I(x)=x,
]

we have

[
I^n(x)=x.
]

Therefore,

[
d(I^n(x),I^n(y))
================

d(x,y).
]

The identity transition preserves perturbations exactly.

It is therefore neutrally stable under the metric-preservation criterion, but not asymptotically stable unless the perturbation is already zero.

---

## 61. Stability and Invertibility

Invertibility does not imply stability.

A transition may possess

[
T^{-1}
]

while perturbations grow under forward iteration.

Likewise, a non-invertible transition may be stable.

Therefore,

[
\text{invertibility}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{stability}.
]

---

## 62. Stability and Equivalence

If two transition systems are equivalent under a structure-preserving transformation that preserves the relevant metric or topology, then their corresponding stability properties may be transferred.

This follows from the preservation of neighborhoods and trajectories under the equivalence transformation.

---

## 63. Topological Stability

When no metric is specified, stability may be formulated using neighborhoods.

Let (U) be a neighborhood of a reference invariant set (A).

A transition is topologically stable near (A) if there exists a neighborhood (V) of (A) such that

[
T^n(V)\subseteq U
]

for all

[
n\geq0.
]

This formulation depends on the topology rather than a particular numerical distance.

---

## 64. Metric Stability

When (S) is a metric space,

[
(S,d),
]

stability is expressed using metric inequalities.

The principal relation is

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))<\epsilon.
]

Metric stability therefore provides quantitative control over perturbations.

---

## 65. Stability and Geometry

If the state space carries geometric structure, the distance function may be induced by the geometry.

For a Riemannian manifold,

[
d_g(x,y)
]

may represent geodesic distance.

Then stability is measured according to

[
d_g(T^n(x),T^n(y)).
]

Thus the stability framework is compatible with the geometric structures of the SMOF state space.

## 66. Stability and Analysis

If transition operators are differentiable or smooth, analytical tools may be used to establish stability.

These include:

* Jacobian analysis;
* spectral analysis;
* Lyapunov functions;
* contraction mappings;
* differential inequalities;
* invariant-region arguments;
* energy estimates.

The selected method must correspond to the regularity available for the transition system.

## 67. Stability and Algebraic Structure

If states and transitions possess algebraic structure, stability may be analyzed through algebraically preserved quantities.

Let

[
G
]

be a group or algebraic structure acting on (S).

If the transition respects the action,

[
T(g\cdot x)=g\cdot T(x),
]

then stability analysis may be reduced through the corresponding symmetry.

Such reductions must preserve the stability criterion.

## 68. Stability and Symmetry

Let a symmetry transformation be

[
g:S\rightarrow S.
]

If

[
g\circ T=T\circ g,
]

then (g) commutes with the transition.

Corresponding stability properties may therefore occur in symmetry-related state structures.

---

## 69. Stability and Conjugate Coordinates

Let

[
z=h(x)
]

be a coordinate transformation.

The transformed transition is

[
T_z=h\circ T\circ h^{-1}.
]

If (h) preserves the required neighborhood structure, stability may be analyzed in either coordinate representation.

The stability classification must be invariant under admissible coordinate changes.

## 70. Stability Requirement for SMOF Runtime

A SMOF runtime implementation claiming stability shall distinguish:

[
\text{mathematical stability}
]

from

[
\text{numerical stability}.
]

Mathematical stability concerns the transition system itself.

Numerical stability concerns the computational representation used to approximate that system.

The two properties shall not be conflated.

---

## 71. Numerical Stability

Let

[
\widehat{T}
]

be a numerical approximation to (T).

Numerical stability concerns the propagation of computational errors

[
e_n
===

d(T^n(x),\widehat{T}^{,n}(x)).
]

A numerically stable implementation prevents uncontrolled amplification of computational error within the specified operating regime.

---

## 72. Mathematical and Computational Separation

SMOF shall preserve the distinction

[
T
\neq
\widehat{T}.
]

The exact mathematical transition is represented by (T).

The implementation is represented by (\widehat{T}).

A mathematical stability certificate shall not automatically be treated as a numerical stability certificate.

---

## 73. Stability Composition Principle

Suppose transitions

[
T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_k
]

each satisfy compatible stability bounds.

The composed transition is

[
T=T_k\circ\cdots\circ T_2\circ T_1.
]

The stability of (T) must be established using the combined perturbation bound.

For contraction constants

[
\lambda_i<1,
]

a corresponding bound is

[
d(T(x),T(y))
\leq
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i\right)d(x,y).
]

Therefore,

[
\lambda_T
=========

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i.
]

---

## 74. Stability and Long-Term Behavior

The long-term behavior of a transition system may be classified through

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(x),A).
]

Possible outcomes include:

[
0,
]

a finite nonzero bound,

[
\infty,
]

or non-convergent behavior.

These outcomes correspond to different stability and attraction regimes.

## 76. Canonical Stability Predicate

For a reference structure (A), define

[
\operatorname{Stable}_T(A)
]

by

[
\forall\epsilon>0,\exists\delta>0:
d(x,A)<\delta
\Rightarrow

for all

For asymptotic stability,

## 80. Stability Theorem

**Theorem 80.1 — Stability by Contraction**

Let ((S,d)) be a metric space and let

[
T:S\rightarrow S
]

satisfy

[
d(T(x),T(y))
\leq
\lambda d(x,y)
]

for some

[
0\leq\lambda<1
]

and all (x,y\in S).

Then

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
\leq
\lambda^n d(x,y).
]

Consequently, perturbations decay exponentially and the transition is exponentially stable over (S).

---

## 81. Proof

Applying the contraction inequality once gives

[
d(T(x),T(y))
\leq
\lambda d(x,y).
]

Applying it repeatedly,

[
d(T^2(x),T^2(y))
\leq
\lambda d(T(x),T(y)).
]

Therefore,

[
d(T^2(x),T^2(y))
\leq
\lambda^2d(x,y).
]

Induction gives

[
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
\leq
\lambda^n d(x,y).
]

Since

[
0\leq\lambda<1,
]

we have

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^n=0.
]

Therefore,

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
d(T^n(x),T^n(y))
=0.
]

Hence the transition is exponentially stable.

---

## 82. Stability Theorem for Fixed Points

**Theorem 82.1**

Let (x^\ast) be a fixed point of (T):

[
T(x^\ast)=x^\ast.
]

If there exist (C>0) and (0<\lambda<1) such that

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)
\leq
C\lambda^n d(x,x^\ast)
]

for all (x) in a neighborhood of (x^\ast), then (x^\ast) is locally exponentially stable.

---

## 83. Proof

Since

[
0<\lambda<1,
]

we have

[
\lambda^n\rightarrow0.
]

Therefore,

[
C\lambda^n d(x,x^\ast)\rightarrow0.
]

Hence,

[
d(T^n(x),x^\ast)\rightarrow0.
]

The exponential bound also implies bounded perturbation propagation.

Therefore (x^\ast) is locally exponentially stable.

---

## 84. Stability Theorem for Attractors

**Theorem 84.1**

Let (A\subseteq S) be invariant and attracting.

If there exists (\delta>0) such that every state satisfying

[
d(x,A)<\delta
]

remains within a prescribed neighborhood of (A) for all (n\geq0), then (A) is locally stable.

If additionally

[
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(T^n(x),A)=0,
]

then (A) is locally asymptotically stable.

---

## 85. Stability Inheritance Principle

If a transition (T) is exponentially stable on a set (A), then any restriction of (T) to an invariant subset

[
B\subseteq A
]

inherits the corresponding perturbation bound, provided the metric and domain assumptions remain valid.

Thus,

[
B\subseteq A
]

may inherit stability from the larger stable domain.

## 86. Stability Non-Inheritance Principle

Stability of a single state does not imply stability of an entire state space.

Likewise,

[
\text{stability of }A
]

does not automatically imply

[
\text{stability of }B
]

when (B) is unrelated to (A).

Each claimed implication must be mathematically established.
